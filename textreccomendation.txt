Building a deep learning model that can intelligently clip videos—such as extracting specific actions, creating highlights, or trimming based on custom durations—requires a stack that handles temporal action localization, efficient data loading, and video processing.

Here are the best open-source packages and frameworks for building this pipeline, broken down by their role.

1. Deep Learning Video Frameworks (Model Training)
These are the core libraries containing pre-built architectures, training loops, and loss functions for video understanding.

MMAction2 (OpenMMLab): The most comprehensive open-source toolbox for video understanding based on PyTorch. It contains heavily optimized implementations for Temporal Action Localization (TAL) and Spatio-temporal Action Detection. If you want a model to learn when an action starts and stops to create a clip, this is the gold standard.

PyTorchVideo (Meta AI): A modular deep learning library specifically designed for video. It excels at fast video data augmentation and clip sampling (e.g., extracting custom 2-second or 5-second clips randomly or uniformly during training), and provides state-of-the-art pre-trained backbones.

Hugging Face transformers: Essential if you want to build a "text-to-clip" model (where a user types a prompt and the model clips the video). It hosts powerful Vision-Language Models (VLMs) tailored for video, such as VideoMAE, VideoCLIP, and X-CLIP.

2. Video I/O & Frame Extraction (Data Pipeline)
To train a model on custom durations, you must efficiently slice videos into tensors during training. Standard video players or generic image loaders are too slow for deep learning.

Decord: A highly optimized video reader backed by FFmpeg. It is specifically built for deep learning pipelines and allows for lightning-fast, random-access frame extraction without needing to decode the entire video.

PyAV: Pythonic bindings for FFmpeg. It provides fine-grained control over audio and video streams, making it perfect for complex frame-level manipulation before feeding data into your model.

TorchVision (Video API): torchvision.io.VideoReader provides a solid native PyTorch method for reading custom temporal clips directly into tensors without external dependencies.

3. Video Editing & Post-Processing (The Output)
Once your trained model predicts the start and end timestamps of a clip, you need a library to actually slice the video file for the end user.

FFmpeg-python: A Python wrapper for the FFmpeg command line. It is the fastest option for slicing videos based on model-predicted timestamps because it can cut streams flawlessly without having to re-encode the entire video.

MoviePy: The go-to Python library for non-linear video editing. You can easily pass the model's output timestamps (e.g., start=12.5s, end=18.0s) into MoviePy to cut, concatenate, add transitions, and export the final video file.

Key Architectures to Look For
When setting up your training pipeline in the frameworks above, search for these specific model architectures depending on your clipping goal:

For Text-Based Clipping: Look into VideoCLIP or ActionCLIP. These map video frames and text into the same space, allowing you to find timestamps matching a specific query.

For Highlight Summarization: Look for LSTM-GANs or Transformer-based Autoencoders, which are often used to score the "importance" of frames to create summaries.

For Fixed-Duration Action Spotting: Look into Actionformer or TimeSformer.
